# MANAN_TRAINING_DEVELOPMENT.md

The blueprint for **TRAINING FOR YOUNG ACTORS**, the illustrated guide, with Manan and
THE BRAIN BRAKE as its case study.

Nothing is built here. This is the plan every later document is made from. Read it whole before
generating a single page or a single sketch.

Written 15.8.2026. Shoot is Tuesday 18.8. He must be ready by Thursday at the latest, so the guide
has to be usable the moment he opens it, on a phone, by a boy who has never acted.

---

## 0. WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS FOR

Two things at once, and they must not fight each other.

**The urgent thing.** Manan has to be able to perform on Tuesday. Everything in the guide must be
usable by a fourteen year old with two days of preparation and no training. If a page cannot be
acted on within five minutes of reading it, that page is decoration and it comes out.

**The lasting thing.** This becomes a book, *Training for Young Actors*, with Manan's preparation
as the worked case study. Other young people entering competitions will use it. That is why the
principles are written as principles and then demonstrated on this film, rather than written only
as instructions for this film.

**The order of authority when they conflict:** Manan being ready on Tuesday wins. Always.

## 1. THE DIAGNOSIS. WHAT ACTUALLY GOES WRONG

Before any technique, the honest list of what a fourteen year old non actor does in front of a
camera. Every section of the guide exists to solve one of these, and any section that solves none
of them should be cut.

**He presents instead of talks.** He hears his own voice go into announcer mode. This is the single
most common failure and it is fatal here, because the whole film depends on him sounding like
somebody working something out.

**He acts the emotion instead of having it.** Told to be curious, he makes a curious face. The face
arrives before the thought and the audience sees the machinery.

**He plays the end of the line at the beginning.** He knows the sentence, so he already knows it
ends in a discovery, and he starts the sentence already having discovered it. The surprise
evaporates.

**He rushes.** Nerves compress time. Two seconds of silence feels to him like twenty. Almost every
line in this film needs a pause he will not naturally give it.

**He does not know what to do with his hands.** So they hang, or fidget, or he folds them, and his
whole body goes dead.

**His eyes drift.** With nothing real to look at, the eyeline wanders and he reads as unfocused or
dishonest, which is the last thing this film can afford.

**He gets worse after take six.** Or better then worse. Young performers peak early and then start
performing their own earlier take.

**He is self conscious about being watched.** Which is the true root of most of the above.

## 2. THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE, TAKEN FROM THE FILM ITSELF

`storytelling.md` says: **empathy before curiosity. Feel, then ask, then learn.**

The same law governs the acting, and this is the spine of the whole guide.

**An actor must feel before he speaks.** If the feeling arrives after the words, the audience gets
words. If the feeling arrives first, the words come out shaped by it and nothing has to be acted at
all.

Which produces the guide's one sentence, the equivalent of "make them feel him before you make them
wonder about him":

> **Do not act the line. Have the thought, and let the line escape.**

Everything else in the book is a method for reliably having the thought.

---

## 3. THE SIX PRINCIPLES, TRANSLATED FROM STORY TO PERFORMANCE

`storytelling.md` has six story medicine principles. Each one has an exact performance equivalent,
and this parallel is what makes the book coherent rather than a bag of tips.

| Story principle | Performance principle |
|---|---|
| Externalise the difficulty | Never play an emotion. Play an object, a person, or a memory. |
| No blame | Never judge your character, and never judge yourself between takes. |
| The resource is already inside | You already contain every feeling this film needs. Nothing is imported. |
| Never state the lesson | Never play the meaning of the line. Let the line mean it. |
| Plant before it is needed | The preparation happens before you roll, not during. |
| End in safety, with a transfer | End every session, and the shoot day itself, calm and finished. |

**The third one is the emotional heart of the book and it is also the film's thesis.** The film says
the reserve was always inside the runner and nothing was added from outside. The guide says the same
thing to Manan about acting: you are not becoming someone else, you are not acquiring a skill you
lack, you are being permitted to use something you already have. *The brain didn't make new energy.
It gave permission.* That sentence is the film's line and it is also the book's dedication page.

This is not a decorative parallel. It is the reason this book can only have been written around this
film.

## 4. THE FIVE DOORS. SENSORY PREPARATION

From `elements.md`. Each element names a register in which a person experiences anything, and if one
is missing the audience feels an absence they cannot name. The same is true inside a performer.

For **every** line in the film, the guide gives Manan a five sense anchor. Not a mood word. A
specific remembered physical fact.

    EARTH   weight      what your body is doing, where the weight sits, what you are touching
    WATER   care        who you care about in this moment, what softens
    FIRE    want        what you want right now, in this second, from the person opposite
    WIND    thought     the exact thought in your head one second before you speak
    ETHER   space       the silence around it, the breath, what you do not say

**Why this works on a fourteen year old specifically.** Told "be curious" he can only guess. Told
"remember the smell of the road after rain, the weight on your right foot, and the fact that you
have just noticed something nobody else has noticed", he has something to actually do. The emotion
is a by product and it arrives on its own.

**The rule for writing these.** Every anchor must be a real memory available to a boy in Bangalore.
Not imagined, not literary. If he cannot answer "when did that happen to you", the anchor is wrong
and must be replaced.

## 5. THE FOUR TOOLS. THE WORKING CORE OF THE BOOK

These are the tools he will actually use on the day. Everything else supports them. Each gets its
own illustrated chapter with a diagram and worked examples.

### 5.1 THE JUST BEFORE

**The single most useful tool for a non actor.** Before any line, answer one question: what happened
one second before this shot started?

Not the scene. The second. He was reaching for the door and it was colder than he expected. He was
about to give up and then the screen flickered. He has just heard something and has not yet decided
what it means.

A performance that begins in the middle of something already happening cannot be a recitation,
because the words are no longer the beginning of anything.

For each line the guide gives the just before in one sentence and never more.

### 5.2 THE SECRET

One private thought he holds and never says. The audience cannot hear it and can always see it.

Example. In frame 3.6, in the hall of tanks, the line is "It was never empty. Somebody closed that
door." The secret underneath is: *and I think it might be closed on me too.* He never says it. It
changes his face.

The secret is what stops a line being only its content. It gives the audience the sense of a person
rather than a narrator, and it is invisible.

### 5.3 THE OBJECT OF ATTENTION

He is never asked to feel. He is always asked to look at something and want something from it.

**This is the whole answer to "acting the emotion".** An emotion aimed at nobody is a face. An
emotion aimed at something is behaviour.

Practically: every setup gets a real physical thing to look at. The tennis ball for Coach Brain's
eyeline. A mark on the wall for the runner. A real key in his hand. Where the drawn world will be
added later he still needs something real, and the guide specifies exactly what, per shot, so
Venkatesh can place it.

### 5.4 THE BREATH

The entry to every state, and the one thing he can control directly when nothing else is working.

Three breath patterns, illustrated, and one is assigned to every line:

    LOW AND SLOW      out longer than in. calm, thinking, arriving. frames 8.1, 3.6, 5.8
    HELD              catch the breath and hold a beat. surprise, noticing. frames 1.6, 5.4
    RECOVERING        after effort, uneven, mouth open. frames 5.6, 5.7

**Why this belongs to this project in particular.** Marko has spent years in nada yoga and knows
that breath is not a metaphor for state, it is the mechanism of state. The film's final image is a
boy breathing with his eyes closed. The guide teaches the same instrument that the film's climax is
made of. Frame 8.1 is not acting. It is the thing itself.

## 6. THE INSTRUMENT. FACE, BODY, EYES, VOICE

Reference chapters, heavily illustrated, that he can look at between takes.

### 6.1 THE FACE

**The principle first, so the sheets are not misused.** These sheets are for recognising, never for
copying. An expression arrived at from outside is a mask. The sheets exist so that when the right
thought produces the right face, he knows what it felt like and can find it again.

**Expression sheet, drawn as one page of the same head, six states, in the film's own pencil style:**

1. Noticing. Something has just caught his attention and he has not decided about it yet.
2. Curious and amused. The 1.6 face. Delighted rather than puzzled.
3. Working it out. Interior, eyes slightly down and off, not looking at anything real.
4. Arriving. The half second when it lands, before he says anything.
5. Delighted rather than shocked. The 3.6 face. This distinction gets its own page.
6. Calm and open. Eyes closed, nothing performed. The 8.1 face.

**The two hardest, both of which get a full page each with a wrong and right pair:**

- **Delighted rather than shocked.** Shock is a wall, delight is a door. A shocked face says the
  world is dangerous, a delighted face says the world is bigger than I thought. This is the
  difference between a film that warns and a film that opens, and it lands on one face in one frame.
- **Not performing at all.** Frame 8.1. The hardest thing on the call sheet and it is not a face at
  all, it is the absence of one.

### 6.2 THE BODY

**Posture sheet, full figure, same pencil style:**

- Weight forward. Interested, leaning in, about to move.
- Weight back. Considering, taking it in, unhurried.
- Weight even, arms down. Neutral. Harder than it sounds and needed for 8.1.
- Caught mid movement. The 5.4 pose, already leaving the frame.
- At the limit. 5.7, the whole body committed.

**The hands.** Their own page, because it is the thing he will most want an answer to.
Rule: give the hands a job or a rest, never a hover. In this film the jobs are the magnifying glass,
the chalk, the phone, the handlebars, the ball, the key. Every shot has one, and that is not an
accident, it is a decision made for him in advance.

### 6.3 THE EYES

**Eyeline diagram, one page, plan view from above** showing camera, lens, eyeline mark, and where
he looks, for each of the film's setups. He should be able to see at a glance where his eyes go in
every shot.

**Three eyelines only, and they must never blur:**

- **To lens.** Talking to one person. Not to an audience, not to a camera. Frames 1.6, 3.6, 8.6.
- **To the mark.** Where a drawn character will be. Frames 4.2, 4.6.
- **Interior.** Off camera and unfocused. Thinking, not looking. Frames 4.6, 5.4.

**The rule that fixes the drift:** to lens means one specific person he chooses in advance and keeps
for the whole film. Not the camera. A friend, standing behind it. Choose them before Tuesday, write
the name down, tell nobody.

### 6.4 THE VOICE

- Speak at the volume of the room, never a presenting volume.
- The pause is part of the line, not a gap in it. Illustrated on the timeline as held frames.
- Down at the end of a statement, not up. Uptalk turns a discovery into a question and makes him
  sound unsure of his own film.
- One sentence per take on the difficult lines. Never string them.
- He never voices the other characters. Coach Brain is cast and recorded already. Manan is Manan.

## 7. LINE BY LINE. THE HEART OF THE BOOK

The largest section and the reason the book exists. **Every line Manan speaks gets one page.**

**Layout of a line page, fixed, and this matters more than anything else in the design:**

    LINE, VERY LARGE                    what he says, and nothing else at this size.
                                        readable at arm's length on a phone.

    small caption under it              to camera / voice over / spoken and on screen

    [ ILLUSTRATION ]                    what he looks like doing it, drawn.
                                        posture, eyeline, hands.

    THE JUST BEFORE      one sentence   what happened one second ago
    THE SECRET           one sentence   what he thinks and never says
    LOOK AT              one line       the real object his eyes go to
    BREATH               one word       low and slow / held / recovering
    THE FIVE             five short     earth water fire wind ether, one anchor each
    TIMING               timecode       length, and where the pause sits
    WATCH OUT FOR        one line       the specific failure this line invites

**The typography rule, stated once and enforced everywhere.** The spoken words are large. Everything
else is small. When he glances at the page between takes he must see only what he has to say. The
direction is for reading beforehand, not during.

**The count.** In the comic strip version his part has grown. Every line must be inventoried from
the current script before pages are built, and the inventory goes in this file before any page is
generated. Estimate on current material: roughly 20 to 26 narration cues plus 6 to 8 to camera
lines, so **28 to 34 line pages**. This is the number that decides the book's length and it must be
confirmed, not assumed.

**The two lines that carry the film get expanded treatment, three pages each:**

- **3.6**, he turns to us in the hall of tanks. The take the judges read him on.
- **8.1**, standing still with his eyes closed, doing nothing at all. The hardest thing on the call
  sheet, and the one that cannot be faked or fixed later.

**Total estimate: 40 to 46 images.** At the measured rate of roughly $0.016 each and two attempts

**The generation rules that already cost us:**

- Name the ceiling, not the direction. Tone needs a bound compared to something in the frame.
- Never use negation. Describe the crop positively.
- Two subjects per frame maximum.
- Reference the single panel, never a multi panel sheet, or the grid is imposed.
- Lettering is composited afterwards, never prompted.
- Every output cropped from 2752x1536 to 2731x1536 before it ships.

## 9. ON THE DAY. THE SHORT SECTION HE ACTUALLY REREADS

Deliberately brief. Two pages, designed to be read on the morning of the shoot.

- Eat before. Nerves and an empty stomach are the same feeling and he will misread one as the other.
- Warm the voice by talking, not by exercises. Ten minutes of ordinary conversation.
- Choose the person you are talking to and keep them all day.
- The first take is a rehearsal that happens to be recorded. Expect nothing of it.
- If a take is wrong, it is the direction that was wrong, not you. Go again without comment.
- Between takes, do not watch yourself and do not review. That is for the evening.
- After take six on any setup, stand up and walk before continuing.
- The silence before you speak belongs to you. Nobody is waiting. Take it.
- When you do not know what to do, breathe out longer than you breathe in and look at the thing.

**The last line of the section, and of the book:**

> Nothing about you needs to change. Only what you believe you already have.

## 10. THE REHEARSAL METHOD, FOR NEHA

One page, for the person directing him at home.

**Shoot every rehearsal on the phone and watch it back together.** There is no better feedback for
an actor than seeing himself. He will correct things on his own that no amount of direction fixes,
because self correction from evidence is the only note that does not feel like criticism.

**The three passes:**

1. **Read it.** Sitting, no performance, just for meaning. Find where he does not understand a word.
2. **Say it.** Standing, to her, as if telling her something. Not to a camera.
3. **Do it.** On the phone, on the mark, with the prop.

Watch back after pass three only. Never after pass one, or he will start self editing before he
understands what he is saying.

**What she should never say:** be more excited, be more natural, put more energy into it. All three
are unactable and all three make him self conscious. **What she should say instead:** what were you
thinking about just then. What do you want from him in this moment. What happened one second before.

## 11. THE CASE STUDY FRAME, FOR THE BOOK VERSION

The published book carries the principles, then Manan's actual preparation as the worked example:
the script pages, the rehearsal notes, what failed, what was changed, and the finished frames beside
them.

**The honesty rule, from `documents.md` and the whole project's practice:** it records what went
wrong as carefully as what went right. A training book in which the student is good at everything
teaches nobody. Where a take failed, the book says which and why.

The site becomes a section of the existing web book alongside *How to Create a Winning Film*, at
`markoboskoauroville.github.io`. Web version only. **No PDF of the book, ever**, per the standing rule.

The training document for Manan himself is a different object: a PDF, because he needs it on paper
and on a phone with no signal, at 25fps timecode, matching the film's own type and colour.

## 12. WHAT MUST NOT HAPPEN

- **No acting theory.** No Stanislavski, no method, no vocabulary he has to learn. Every instruction
  is a physical action or a real memory.
- **No mood words as direction.** Be curious, be excited, be natural are all forbidden in the pages
  themselves. They are the failure this book exists to prevent.
- **Nothing that makes him self conscious.** Any note about how he appears rather than what he is
  doing is cut.
- **No praise inflation.** He will be told plainly when something is not working, kindly and
  specifically, because a boy who is told everything is wonderful stops trusting the feedback and
  then cannot improve.
- **Not too long.** 25 to 30 pages is right. Forty is a book he will not read before Tuesday, and
  the urgent thing wins.
- **Do not let the book become about the writer.** It is his film.

One

The question a fourteen year old asked

Manan wanted to know where the last sprint comes from. A marathon runner has been empty for an hour. Then, in sight of the line, something arrives that was not supposed to exist.

For a century the answer was the muscles. They run out of fuel, they run out of air, they stop. It is a tidy explanation and it is taught everywhere. It is also, at minimum, incomplete, because when scientists tested runners at the exact moment they gave up, the muscles could still do far more.

So the interesting question is not why we stop. It is who decides.

The film he proposed answers that with one influential model, the Central Governor Theory, which holds that the brain sets the limit in advance and always keeps something in reserve. Fatigue, on this account, is not a report that the body has failed. It is an instruction to slow down before real danger begins.

He chose the subject, the science and the theory. That has not changed through any version of the film, and it should not.

Two

The first script was too good to keep

His original script runs twenty four pages. It has a muscle factory with tiny workers, a mission control room, a coach, a lever, a case that gets stamped CLOSED, and a genuinely lovely closing voice over.

Read aloud at a natural pace it is about five minutes long.

Every good line in it was a real line. The problem was never quality. The problem is that a two minute film holds roughly two hundred and forty spoken words, and the script had far more than that, all of them doing work.

Cutting somebody else's good writing is the least glamorous and most useful job on a short film.

What survived was the shape rather than the sentences. The factory became a blackboard. Five lines of the old theory became one chalk column. A speech about scientific method became a look on a boy's face.

Three

Two minutes is not a target, it is a door

The competition measures running time on the video platform's own clock. A film that runs over is not marked down. It is not judged at all.

That single fact governs every other decision in this production. It is why the film is timed in frames rather than minutes, why no line can be added without another coming out, and why the first honest assembly was a crisis rather than a draft.

The number that changed everything

2:47

The first honest timing came to two minutes and forty seven seconds. Forty seven seconds over a limit that disqualifies rather than penalises.

Everything since has been subtraction. The film now runs 1:57.80: fifty panels of picture and two and a fifth seconds of margin. The bar at the top of this page is that margin, drawn to scale.

Four

Three shapes before the right one

Version two was a conventional short. A live action boy composited into fully animated worlds, thirty five shots, a great deal of motion.

Version three kept the ambition and moved the science earlier.

Version four threw out the motion.

The film became a comic strip. Fifty still panels, some held for as little as ten frames, cut in rhythm rather than animated in sequence. The camera does not move because in most of the film there is no camera. What moves is your eye, panel to panel, the way it moves down a page.

The reason was arithmetic before it was art. A held panel costs a fraction of an animated shot, which lets a two minute film carry fifty images instead of fifteen. But the form turned out to suit the subject, because a comic strip is how a fourteen year old thinks about a mystery, and this is a detective story.

Five

Photograph what is true, draw what is thought

Every frame obeys one rule, and the rule is the argument.

Anything real is a photograph. The boy is a photograph. The blackboard he writes on is a real blackboard and the chalk in his hand is real chalk. The evidence is photographed.

Anything that is an idea is a drawing. The muscle factory is a drawing. The runner is a drawing. The coach inside your head is a drawing. Theories are drawn in graphite on cream paper, because a theory is something somebody sketched.

The two layers are not kept apart. The photograph sits on the paper and casts a real shadow onto it, so the boy is physically inside the drawing rather than pasted over it. Where the worlds touch, the shadow does the work.

the magnifying glass is how we cross between them. twice in the film, the picture travels through the lens.

Six

Drawing fifty panels with a machine

All of the drawn material was generated, then corrected, then corrected again. That produced a set of rules that read like superstition and are simply true.

Saying no summons the thing. Asking for a frame with no crowd in it reliably produces a crowd. You describe what is in the picture, never what is absent.

A frame holds about two subjects. Ask for three and one is silently dropped, usually the one that mattered.

Never show it a labelled picture. A frame carrying a review note was once used as a reference, and the note was faithfully redrawn into the artwork. That happened twice before the rule got written down.

Lead with the photograph. In a frame where a real boy stands in a drawn world, if the drawing is shown first the boy comes back drawn, which destroys the one thing that frame exists to prove.

None of this was theory. Each line is the scar from a specific afternoon.

Seven

Casting a voice for a thought

Two characters in this film are not people. One is the coach in the brain. One is a worker in a factory that does not exist, standing in for a theory that has run out.

They are synthesised, and cast the way you would cast actors. The coach is a warm British teacher, amused and entirely unembarrassed. The factory worker is a tired American at the end of a long shift, flat and finished. The contrast is deliberate: the explanation sounds like a teacher, and the old theory sounds like a closing plant.

The performances are directed rather than typed. Each line carries an instruction written the way you would say it to an actor, and the same sentence with and without direction gives two completely different readings.

Manan is never synthesised. Every word he speaks is his own voice, recorded on the day, including all the narration. The competition is judging a boy explaining something, so a boy explaining something is what has to be on the soundtrack.

Eight

One file that is the film

A single file describes all fifty panels: what is in each one, who speaks, what they say, how many frames it holds, and whether that duration is measured from a real recording or estimated from a word count.

Everything else is generated from it. The film document, the presenter's acting book, the camera guide, the animator's packages. Nothing is typed twice.

That sounds like housekeeping. It is actually what keeps the film honest, because the acting book is built from the same source as the film. The boy cannot rehearse a line that is not in the picture, and the picture cannot quietly drift from what he rehearsed.

Nine · the day the pictures lied

“It hasn't changed.”

Frames get replaced in place, keeping the same filename. Somewhere in the chain, something remembered pictures by their name instead of their contents. So a corrected frame was regenerated, saved, published, and the old one kept being served.

The director said three times that the picture had not changed. Three times he was told it must be his download, then a cache somewhere, then his viewer. All three answers were wrong and the file was wrong. Hours went into explaining away a measurement that was correct.

Two rules came out of it and they now sit at the top of the production notes. Cache on the contents of a file, never on its name. And when somebody says a file has not changed, treat it as a measurement: download the published file and compare it before offering any other explanation.

Ten

The documents started answering back

Two days before the shoot the cinematographer read the camera guide properly, which is more than most people do with a document handed to them, and found two things wrong with it.

The written instruction said the key light comes from camera left. Every reference frame in the film shows the light coming from camera right. He asked which one to follow.

He was right, and the written rule had been wrong for months. Worse, it cited another document as its authority, and that document names no side at all. It only says the light must be hard enough to cast a real shadow and must never change sides. Somebody had invented a side and then attributed it upward, and nobody had checked the sentence against a picture.

Then a second, larger one. The camera guide had grown its own list of camera setups while the film document already had one, and the two disagreed about where six panels were shot. One said a grey backdrop. The other said the boy's own room. He caught that the day before the camera rolled.

A rule that is written down and never measured against the artefact will outlive the artefact, and be handed to everyone who reads it.

Both documents now read one shared file, so they cannot disagree again. The answer to his question is printed inside the guide as well: follow the picture for anything you can see, follow the words for anything you cannot, and say which one was wrong.

Eleven

The producer read it properly

Neha went back through the original script line by line, marked everything she could not find in the current film, and asked why.

Almost all of it was there, in a different shape. The old theory had become chalk on a board. A speech had become a factory worker saying that's it, we're done, and a giant stamp coming down. Lines had moved from the boy to the coach so that he could react instead of explain.

But three things were genuinely gone, and one of her notes was the sharpest in the production. The theory is named in the film and never actually explained out loud. For a science competition the audience should hear the definition once, in the presenter's own voice.

She was right. Restoring what she asked for cost about thirteen seconds against a margin of two, so three moments came out to pay for it, including a beat about how new ideas get accepted that everybody liked. The ending was rebuilt as the three line sequence from the original draft.

Twelve

Nine setups, one boy, one grey wall

There is one shooting day. No runner, no crowd, no factory, no coach. One fourteen year old, and everything else arrives later.

Twenty seven panels need a lens. They break into nine setups: a grey backdrop for anything that gets cut out and placed inside a drawing, a white void for the end of the film, his own room at his desk, at a real blackboard and on a stationary bike, a recording booth, and one exterior on a quiet road at dawn.

The presenter has ADHD and the whole method is built around that. One sentence per take. The camera keeps rolling through the resets, because the unguarded moment between takes is often the one that gets used. No counting down. Breaks every twenty minutes. Take six is usually the one.

He rehearses from a book that gives him one line per page, with the moment before it, the thing he is not saying, where to look, how to breathe and where the silence goes. That book is generated from the same file as the film, so nothing he learns can quietly change underneath him.

Fourteen

What is left

Three phases, in order, each blocked by the one before it.

Edit

Assembling the stripThe footage is cut against the panels and the film finds its real length. Everything downstream waits here, because one line moving changes every timecode after it.

Animation

The layers come aliveThe animator receives the boy already cut out with an alpha channel, the layered artwork, the audio and a locked edit, and spends his days on animation, compositing and finishing rather than preparation. The preparation is the director's job, and doing it properly is what lets the animator spend every hour on the part only he can do.

Sound

Music and sound designNot started, and roughly half the film. Percussion for the factory, a single sustained tone for the coach, and a three note motif introduced in the first twenty seconds that does not resolve until the runner crosses the line.

Then it is a two minute film about the thing that stops you, and whether it was ever really a wall.

# THE BRAIN BRAKE
### The book the film was made from

## PART TWO — THE HOUSE OF THE BODY

### Chapter Nine — The Passage

There is a moment, when a body has been asked for everything it has, that the
asking stops.

Manan had been running. Not the running of a race, which has an end and a ribbon
and someone to beat, but the running of a boy who wanted to know something and had
no other way to ask. He had put the question to his legs on a bicycle, and to his
lungs on a hill, and to the whole of himself on the long road past the water tank,
and every time the answer had come back the same, in the same flat voice, at the
same place: no further, not today, that is enough.

He had never believed it. That was his difficulty and it would turn out to be his
gift. He could feel, somewhere under the burning, that there was more left. Not
much. But some. And the more was being held back by something that was not his
legs.

So on the last afternoon he ran until the voice said no, and then he kept going,
and the world did the strange courteous thing it does when you refuse it. It
folded.

He closed his eyes. The dark did not come.

What came instead was a turning.

Around him, or perhaps through him, a throat had opened in the air, pale as paper
and wide as a room, and it went up. It did not run straight back like a corridor.
It narrowed the way water narrows when it leaves a basin, curving in on itself,
drawing its own walls toward its own centre, except that the narrow end of it was
somewhere above and everything in it was climbing.

And the water it was made of was made of keys.

Not lying in the water. Being the water. Every strand of that rising current was a
line of small keys laid end to end, tip to head, tip to head, strung out loosely
with gaps between them so that each strand was more air than key, and there were
not many strands. The whole vortex was sketched rather than built, a few long
curving lines climbing and turning around a mostly empty middle. All of them the
same key: a long round shaft, a flat disc at the head with three lobes cut clean
through it and a small number five stamped near the edge, three raised rings at the
collar, three square teeth at the bit. Near him one or two were exact enough to
read. Further off they were only marks.

Not one of them was gold.

This is worth saying slowly, because it is the whole of what happened to him. He
was being carried up through a river made of the very thing he was looking for, and
it was grey. Grey as the pencil the world was drawn in. He passed within a hand's width of
ten thousand keys and did not see a single one, because he did not yet know that a
key was what he needed. The thing you are looking for is invisible until you know
you are looking for it. Everyone learns this eventually. He learned it at speed, going
up.

And then it took him, and it did not take him down.

He went up. That was the part he had not expected and the part he could never
afterwards explain to anybody. A whirlpool is supposed to pull you under. This one
lifted. It closed around him and drew him upward the way water is drawn up a stem,
and his feet left whatever he had been standing on, and everything he was wearing
pulled downward while he went the other way.

The turning rolled him onto his side as it carried him, arms out, and for a moment
he was looking back down the way he had come, at the wide bright mouth of it far
below, with the ordinary afternoon still visible through it and already too far.
Then the current turned him again and he rose past us with his face open, and the
keys streamed by underneath him like rain falling upward.

He did not rise for long. It only felt long, the way it does.

### Chapter Ten — The House

He arrived standing, which is its own small mercy.

The house was white, and it was breathing.

Not obviously. Not so that you could point at it. But the walls had the soft
swelling of clay pressed by hands rather than cut by tools, and they rose and
settled, rose and settled, so slowly that you would have to stand still for a
minute to be sure. Everything was the colour of old paper. There was no sky. There
was no outside at all, once the door had closed behind him, and he had not seen it
close.

He understood, in the way you understand things in that state, without being told
and without surprise, that he was inside himself.

Not inside his body the way a surgeon would mean it. There were no wet things here,
nothing red, nothing that would frighten a child. It was a house. It had corridors
and vaults and doors and a floor worn smooth down the middle by long use, and the
long use was his own, and he had been walking on it since the day he was born
without ever once looking down.

And he had come here to find something.

He did not know what. He only knew the shape of the not knowing, which is the same
shape as a key, though he could not have told you that either.

### Chapter Eleven — The Doors

The corridor was full of doors, and every door had a swelling in it.

Not a sign. Not a word. Nobody in this house had ever needed to read. The clay
itself rose into the shape of what waited behind it, the way a tree is moulded into
the wall of an old house by the hands that built it, so that you run your palm over
it in the dark and know.

The first door carried a bundle of fibres, a muscle drawn as a rope of gathered
strands, and it stood a little open, because he had already tried it.

Inside was a long hall of tendon and cord, pale and warm and quiet. Ropes ran the
length of it and disappeared into the walls at both ends. This was the part of him
that had been shouting all afternoon, and now that he was standing in it, it had
nothing to say. It was simply here, and it was fine, and it had been fine the whole
time. Nothing was torn. Nothing was finished. There was no key.

At the far end of that hall, small and unhurried, another door waited, and on it a
pair of lungs.

The bellows hall was the largest room in the house. Great pale bulbs hung from the
vaulting and swelled and emptied without a sound, and the air moved past his face
in long slow tides, and he stood in the middle of it and breathed with it because
there was nothing else to do. This was the part that had burned. It was not
burning. It had never actually been in danger. There was no key here either.

At the end of the bellows hall, a heart.

The heart hall was chambered and dim and it thudded, and the thud was the sound he
had been hearing in his own ears for the last mile of the road. Standing inside it,
it was not urgent at all. It was patient. It was the most patient thing he had ever
been near. It had been doing this since before he existed and it intended to
continue long after this afternoon was forgotten.

And there was no key in it.

He had now been through the three parts of himself that had told him to stop, and
not one of them had been the thing that stopped him.

That is the discovery. Everything after it is only arriving.

At the end of the heart hall, at the vanishing point, where the corridor closed to
a single small shape, there was a door with something on it that he had not seen
anywhere in this house.

### Chapter Twelve — The Key Door

It was a key.

Not a plaque, not a name, not a word. A key raised in the clay, running the whole
height of the door from the floor to the top of the arch, the same white as the wall
it swelled out of, so that it was not decoration and not a label but simply the door
telling the truth about itself in the only language this house had.

By now he could read it. So can you. Nobody explained the bumps and nobody needed
to, and that is why this door works and a written sign would not have.

He put his hand on it.

And behind it, in a small round room, at a curved desk of pale clay covered in dials
and switches and little grey screens, in a chair much too big for him, sat a brain.

He was quite small. He had a cheerful, tired, competent face, the face of somebody
who has been on shift a very long time and has stopped expecting visitors. He turned
round when the door opened, and he did not look startled, and he did not look
guilty, which was somehow worse, because it meant he had been doing his job.

And on a cord around his neck, catching a light that came from nowhere in that room,
hung the only gold thing in the world.

## THE LAST PAGE

There is one more thing, and it belongs at the end because it was at the end.

Long before the running, before the bicycle and the hill and the long road past
the water tank, before anyone had thought of a house made of clay or a key with
three teeth, there was a habit older than any of it. A plate of food is not eaten
first and offered afterwards. It is offered, and then it is eaten. The giving comes
before the having. Nobody who does this can quite explain what it is for, and
everybody who does it knows.

So this film was made and then given away.

It is dedicated to Ganesha, who removes obstacles, and it is worth saying why that
is the right hands to put it in and not merely a pleasant one. The whole of what
happens here is a boy discovering that the thing standing across his road was never
a wall. It was a setting. Someone had put it there, carefully, in good faith, to
keep him safe, and it could be moved. A film about an obstacle that turns out to be
removable has only one place to go when it is finished.

None of this is mentioned earlier. Not once. There is no hint in the first frame
and no echo in the last shot, and if you watch the whole thing twice you will find
nothing that was pointing at it.

That is deliberate, and it is the difference between a theme and a gift. A theme is
prepared for. A gift arrives.

So it comes at the very end, after every name, when the film has stopped being
something to look at: a single card, an elephant headed figure forming out of the
paper the way everything in this story forms out of paper, and a line under him
saying who this was for.

Then the music, and then nothing.
